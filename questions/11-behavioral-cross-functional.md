# 11. Behavioral & Cross-Functional Questions

Assessing collaboration, judgment, and communication — critical for a role sitting at the intersection of aging biology, computational modeling, biostatistics, and drug discovery, in a field with a well-documented history of hype outpacing evidence.

---

### 11.1 Tell me about a time you had to push back on an exciting-sounding finding or claim — your own, a colleague's, or an external source's — because you weren't confident the underlying evidence actually supported it. How did you handle it?

**What interviewers look for:** Given the entire evidentiary rigor theme threaded throughout this repo (particularly categories 1.20, 5.12, and 9.8-9.9), this is arguably the single most important behavioral trait for this specific field — evidence the candidate can resist the genuine, well-documented pull toward overstatement that this field's hype-cycle history creates, while still communicating in a way that's constructive rather than reflexively dismissive of genuinely promising early findings.

---

### 11.2 Describe a time you had to evaluate a computational model's output with genuine skepticism, checking its validation status and underlying assumptions rather than accepting a confident-looking prediction at face value. What did you specifically check?

**What interviewers look for:** Directly probing the model validation discipline discussed throughout categories 3 and 8 — strong answers describe specific verification steps (checking sensitivity analysis, applicability domain, external validation status) rather than a vague general claim of being careful with models.

---

### 11.3 Tell me about a time you worked closely with people from a genuinely different professional background than your own (e.g., a wet-lab biologist working with a computational modeler, or a biostatistician working with a drug discovery chemist). What made that collaboration effective or difficult?

**What interviewers look for:** Given how genuinely interdisciplinary this role is — spanning aging biology, computational/systems modeling, biostatistics, and drug discovery chemistry — real experience and skill bridging these different professional cultures and vocabularies, with genuine curiosity about and respect for each discipline's specific priorities and constraints.

---

### 11.4 How do you personally stay current with a field that spans molecular aging biology, computational modeling methodology, and clinical trial design — three areas that typically have quite different literatures, conferences, and professional communities?

**What interviewers look for:** A genuine, sustainable strategy for continued interdisciplinary learning — strong answers describe specific habits (which journals, conferences, or professional communities they follow across these different areas) rather than a vague general claim of staying current, and ideally show awareness that genuine competence in this role requires deliberate, ongoing attention across genuinely distinct domains.

---

### 11.5 Describe a time a hypothesis or model prediction you were genuinely excited about didn't hold up once tested more rigorously or replicated independently. How did you handle it, and what did you learn?

**What interviewers look for:** Genuine scientific accountability and comfort with negative or disappointing results — particularly important given how often rigorous evaluation in this field (per categories 5.12, 6.12, and 10.2) legitimately reveals that an exciting early finding doesn't survive more rigorous scrutiny — strong answers show the candidate can communicate this kind of outcome clearly and constructively rather than downplaying it or becoming defensive.

---

### 11.6 Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex modeling or aging biology concept to a non-technical stakeholder (an investor, a business development colleague, or a board member). How did you make it accessible without oversimplifying to the point of overstatement?

**What interviewers look for:** Clear communication paired with genuine technical integrity — evidence the candidate can translate complexity (the distinction between correlation and causation in aging biomarkers, or between a model's predictions and established fact) for a non-expert audience without crossing into the kind of overstatement this field's commercial pressures can encourage, particularly relevant given the conflict-of-interest dynamics discussed in category 9.9.

---

### 11.7 Tell me about a time you had to make a recommendation or decision with genuinely incomplete evidence — for example, evaluating a candidate target with limited model organism data, or a novel biomarker without extensive validation. How did you approach that uncertainty?

**What interviewers look for:** Structured reasoning under genuine scientific uncertainty, a routine condition in this still-maturing field — strong answers describe a deliberate process (being explicit about the specific evidentiary tier a given finding sits at, per the frameworks discussed throughout categories 1, 2, and 5, and proposing next steps calibrated to that tier) rather than either false confidence or an inability to move forward.

---

### 11.8 How would you handle a situation where a supervisor, investor, or commercial stakeholder wanted you to characterize a compound's evidence or a model's validation status more confidently than you believed was actually warranted?

**What interviewers look for:** Directly probing the evidence-integrity themes discussed throughout categories 1, 5, 6, and 9 — strong answers describe specific, professional approaches to this tension (proposing accurate alternative framing, being willing to have a direct conversation about the evidentiary gap and its real scientific and reputational stakes) rather than either capitulating to overstatement or refusing to engage constructively with the stakeholder's underlying business pressure.

---

### 11.9 Tell me about a time you had to coordinate work across multiple specialized functions (e.g., wet-lab biology, computational modeling, biostatistics, clinical operations) on a project with different priorities or timelines. How did you keep things aligned?

**What interviewers look for:** Real project coordination experience relevant to this field's genuinely multi-disciplinary workflow (echoing the end-to-end pipeline discussed in category 10.1) — strong answers describe specific communication and coordination practices rather than a generic claim of being "a good communicator."

---

### 11.10 Describe a time you had to advocate for additional replication, validation, or a more conservative interpretation despite pressure (timeline, funding, or team enthusiasm) to move forward more quickly with a promising-looking finding. What was the outcome?

**What interviewers look for:** Willingness to hold a rigorous evidentiary line even under real pressure, particularly important in a field this prone to premature excitement — strong answers describe a specific, concrete situation and how the tension was navigated, ideally including how they made a persuasive, evidence-based case rather than simply asserting caution was necessary.

---

### 11.11 What draws you specifically to longevity therapeutics as opposed to more conventional drug discovery or computational biology work? How do you think about the genuine tension between this field's ambitious, exciting potential and its current, much more modest state of rigorously established evidence?

**What interviewers look for:** Authentic motivation paired with realistic, clear-eyed awareness of the field's genuine current limitations (echoing the honest evidence-tier calibration threaded throughout this entire repo) — candidates expressing only uncritical enthusiasm for the field's ambitious framing, without genuine appreciation for how much current evidence actually supports versus how much remains hypothesis, tend to be a weaker fit than those who can articulate genuine excitement alongside real scientific humility about where the field currently stands.

---

### 11.12 Tell me about a time you received critical feedback on your work — a flawed model assumption, an overstated conclusion, a methodological gap — that was hard to hear. How did you respond, and did it change your approach going forward?

**What interviewers look for:** Genuine capacity to receive and productively act on critical feedback without defensiveness — particularly relevant given how much this field's scientific rigor depends on colleagues and reviewers being willing and able to give and receive exactly this kind of challenging review (echoing the peer review scenario discussed in category 10.3).

---

### 11.13 Describe your approach to staying appropriately skeptical of an exciting new finding, target, or technology in this field, while still remaining genuinely open to real, valuable scientific progress. How do you personally calibrate that balance, given how often this field has swung between overstated hype and legitimate, hard-won progress?

**What interviewers look for:** Directly probing the evaluation discipline modeled throughout this entire repo, particularly categories 1.20, 5.12, and 9.8 — strong answers describe specific personal habits or checks (tracing a claim back to its actual evidentiary tier, checking for independent replication, being alert to conflicts of interest) rather than simply claiming general skepticism or general enthusiasm without a concrete, balanced process behind it.

---

### 11.14 Tell me about a time you had to balance moving a project or analysis forward efficiently against wanting to more thoroughly validate an underlying model, biomarker, or biological claim. How did you decide where to draw that line?

**What interviewers look for:** Evidence of proportionate, risk-based judgment — a strong candidate describes distinguishing between situations where deeper validation was clearly warranted given the actual scientific and resource stakes involved, versus situations where a reasonable, well-justified level of confidence was sufficient to proceed efficiently, rather than either always defaulting to maximal caution regardless of stakes, or moving forward without appropriate scrutiny regardless of what's genuinely at stake for the program's scientific credibility and resource allocation.
